HOW JAPAN BECAME THE ANIME CAPITAL OF THE WORLD. VOICEOVER SCRIPT (DRAFT FOR APPROVAL)
=====================================================================================
Narrator: Kokoro af_heart, speed 0.92 (Batang 90s pacing). Narration starts at 4.6 s after the
Papel Tales title card; the video ends on the full-frame title card (v16, v18).
Length: about 4 minutes, 10 scenes, 32 lines. About 2-3 meaningful visuals per line (v16).
Jokes: 10 [JOKE]. Sound effects: 2-3 soft ones at most. Zoom-ins: 2 (marked [ZOOM]).

THEME BRIEF (v13)
The whole video looks like an animator's desk coming to life. Each chapter opens as a sheet of
paper sliding onto a backlit LIGHT TABLE with peg holes at the top (the "EP" cards of the K-drama
video are replaced by numbered animation sheets: "SHEET 01 ... 1917"). Scenes are built from
stacked transparent CELS: background painting at the back, characters on cels in front, so the
depth layers literally are animation cels with soft shadows between them. Manga touches: screentone
dots, speech balloons, panel borders (used sparingly). The era of each scene sets its paper:
sepia film-strip paper (1917), inked manga pages (1950s), black-and-white TV scanlines (1963),
neon on black construction paper (1980s), glossy trading-card colours (1990s), soft watercolour
(Oscar night), bright phone-screen glow (today).
Running gag: THE MARGIN DOODLE. A tiny stick-figure doodle lives in the corner of a school notebook.
In every era it gets "redrawn" in that era's style: wobbly 1917 lines -> big manga eyes -> a little
robot-style boy -> spiky 80s hair -> cute round 90s style -> modern sparkle eyes. It reacts to each
scene (cheers, cries, covers its ears at the explosion). In the epilogue it finally moves on its own.

SCRIPT, SHOTS AND BEATS (shot type in brackets; beats are the 2-3 things that matter)

--- PROLOGUE ---  (notebook on a desk; world map behind)
Line 1. Right now, somewhere in Brazil, a kid is drawing giant sparkly eyes in a school notebook.
   [wide] notebook on desk from the start; pencil draws two huge sparkly eyes; the margin doodle waves
Line 2. In Paris, someone is dressed as a ninja on the subway. In Manila, a whole family is arguing about which anime to watch tonight. [JOKE]
   [medium] pins light up on the world map: Paris (ninja on a subway seat, everyone ignores him) then Manila (family tugging one remote)
Line 3. Most of these stories come from one country: Japan. So how did a small island nation become the anime capital of the world?
   [wide] map pin on Japan glows; a paper crown floats down onto Japan

--- 1917: THE FIRST CARTOONS ---  (silent cinema, sepia film-strip paper)
Line 4. Our story begins in nineteen seventeen, in Tokyo, when movies were silent and storytellers called benshi spoke over the films.
   [wide] 1917 sheet slides onto the light table; cinema with flickering screen; the benshi talks with big hand gestures
Line 5. That year, a few young artists drew the very first Japanese cartoons by hand, frame by frame.
   [insert] a hand draws on paper sheets that flip like a flipbook; the doodle becomes wobbly 1917-style
Line 6. One that still survives is called Namakura Gatana, The Blunt Sword. It is about a samurai who buys a terrible sword and loses every fight. [JOKE]
   [medium] on the cinema screen: a paper samurai swings a bendy sword that flops like a noodle; audience laughs
Line 7. It lasts about two minutes. Making it took months. [JOKE]
   [two panels] left: a tiny clock "2 min"; right: a calendar flipping fast, the artist's beard growing

--- THE GOD OF MANGA ---  (Tezuka's studio at night, inked manga pages)
Line 8. After the war, a young doctor named Osamu Tezuka decided he would rather draw comics than treat patients. [JOKE]
   [medium] a man in glasses and a beret sets down a stethoscope and picks up a pen; the doodle gets big manga eyes
Line 9. He filled his manga with movie tricks: close-ups, dramatic angles, and big, expressive eyes.
   [panels] three manga panels fill in: a close-up, a tilted angle, a pair of huge shining eyes
Line 10. Fans called him the God of Manga, and editors sometimes waited outside his door all night for his pages. [JOKE]
   [wide] the hallway outside fills with sleepy editors; a clock moves to morning; one page slides under the door

--- THE FIRST COLOR FEATURE ---  (1958 studio of cel painters)
Line 11. Meanwhile, in nineteen fifty-eight, the studio Toei released The Tale of the White Serpent, one of Japan's first full-length color animated films.
   [wide] painters colour cels in rows; a white paper serpent glides along the film reel and into colour

--- ASTRO BOY ON TV ---  (1963 living room, black-and-white TV)
Line 12. Then, on New Year's Day, nineteen sixty-three, Tezuka's Astro Boy flew onto Japanese television.
   [wide] family on tatami; the TV switches on; a little robot-style boy flies across the screen; the doodle becomes a robot boy
Line 13. A weekly cartoon was very expensive, so his team got clever. They reused drawings, moved only the mouths, and slid pictures across the screen.
   [insert] the same drawing is stamped again and again; only a paper mouth flaps; a picture slides on a cel
Line 14. This cheaper style, called limited animation, became part of the anime look. Sometimes, being short on money is the mother of invention. [JOKE]
   [medium] a piggy bank with three coins shrugs; a light bulb pops on above it
Line 15. Just eight months later, Astro Boy was flying on American TV too.
   [wide] a paper airplane path flies from Japan across the Pacific to a US TV set

--- THE SECRET INGREDIENT ---  (printing press and newsstand)
Line 16. Here is Japan's secret ingredient: manga.
   [wide] printing press rolls out comic pages; a stack of thick weekly magazines lands on a newsstand
Line 17. Weekly comic magazines tried out thousands of stories, readers voted for their favorites, and the winners got to move. [JOKE]
   [medium] readers drop ballots into a box; the winning comic panel peels off the page and starts to animate

--- THE EIGHTIES ---  (neon city street at night)
Line 18. In the nineteen eighties, home video arrived, and creators made bolder stories for older fans.
   [wide] neon street; a video shop window glows; older fans (teens, a man in a suit) line up
Line 19. In nineteen eighty-eight, Akira roared onto movie screens, and audiences overseas realized cartoons were not just for kids. [ZOOM]
   [push-in, zoom 1 of 2] the marquee lights up "AKIRA"; a red paper motorbike streaks past with a light trail; the doodle gets spiky hair and covers its ears

--- THE NINETIES ---  (US school lunch table, 1999)
Line 20. Then came the nineties, and a little yellow electric mouse took over playgrounds everywhere.
   [wide] lunch table; a yellow lightning-bolt card glows (no copyrighted character drawn); the doodle turns cute and round
Line 21. Kids traded cards at lunch, sang the theme song, and tried to catch them all, while parents tried to figure out what a Pokemon even was. [JOKE]
   [medium] cards fly between hands; a parent stares at a card upside down, then sideways

--- OSCAR NIGHT ---  (red carpet, soft watercolour)
Line 22. In two thousand three, Hayao Miyazaki's Spirited Away won the Oscar for Best Animated Feature.
   [wide] red carpet, camera flashes; a gold statue rises on a stand; "2003" stamp
Line 23. A Japanese film about a girl working in a bathhouse for spirits had won one of Hollywood's biggest prizes. [JOKE]
   [medium] a paper bathhouse appears behind the statue; a polite spirit in a mask claps; the doodle cries happy tears

--- ANIME EVERYWHERE ---  (globe of phone screens)
Line 24. Today, anime streams to fans in almost every country on Earth.
   [wide] the paper globe spins; phone screens light up all around it
Line 25. In two thousand twenty, Demon Slayer: Mugen Train became the highest-grossing film ever in Japan.
   [medium] a paper steam train with a green-and-black checker pattern rolls across the globe; box-office ticket stubs pile up
Line 26. And in twenty twenty-three, Japan's anime industry earned about three point three trillion yen, more than twenty billion US dollars, and over half of it came from fans outside Japan.
   [insert] a coin tower grows; a pie chart fills past the halfway line in "overseas" colour

--- SO WHY JAPAN? ---  (the light table, all the era sheets stacked)
Line 27. So why Japan? First, the stories: manga gave anime a giant library of ideas.
   [wide] the manga stack from scene 6 slides in and grows into a tall library
Line 28. Second, the style: artists learned to do a lot with a little, and created a look anyone can recognize.
   [insert] all the doodle versions stand in a row, from wobbly 1917 to sparkly today, and bow
Line 29. And third, anime was never only for kids. There is a story for everyone: robots, romance, sports, cooking, and even volleyball. [JOKE]
   [panels] five quick manga panels; the last one is a dramatic close-up of a volleyball

--- EPILOGUE ---  (back to the notebook in Brazil)
Line 30. Back in Brazil, the kid finishes the drawing and flips the corner of the notebook.
   [medium] same notebook and desk as the prologue; thumb flips the page corner
Line 31. The little doodle in the margin starts to move. [ZOOM]
   [slow push-in, zoom 2 of 2] the margin doodle walks, waves and jumps off the page
Line 32. And just like that, the next anime story begins.
   [wide] the doodle lands on the light table; the full-frame Papel Tales title card fades in and holds

TOTALS: 32 lines, about 80 beats, 10 jokes, 1 running gag (the margin doodle), 2 zooms.
